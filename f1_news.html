Max Verstappen jokes last time he won a race like F1 Las Vegas GP it was "Mario Kart"

Four-time Formula 1 champion Max Verstappen says the last time he won a race like the Las Vegas Grand Prix was when he was playing Mario Kart .

The Red Bull driver converted pole position into his 75th grand prix win, and his third in Las Vegas, in a race that featured 352 overtakes and 35 lead changes.

Asked during the post-race press conference when he had last won a race like it, the Dutchman was reluctant to answer at first: "I probably shouldn't say it. I probably should not. I remember winning one like this when I was playing Mario Kart .

"Sometimes you drop back and you get drafts, and there's like a catch-up, you know? Always when you're dropping back, you go faster. So that's the last time that I felt something like this," he said.

The Red Bull driver was passed for the lead time and again, mostly by Mercedes' George Russell , and hit back every time. While he managed to retake the lead, he was not able to extend his advantage and break away from the pack behind him.

"It was just the tow combination with the deployment. Of course, when you are within a second, you have the overtake mode as well, and around here that is powerful. So, yeah, you could not pull away."

Photo by: James Sutton / LAT Images via Getty Images

Asked whether he had enjoyed the race around the streets of Las Vegas, Verstappen drew a line between the driver and the spectator.

"When I look at it from my racer heart, no. When you look at it as a show, spectacle, yes. I've enjoyed my repassing and everything, but I prefer to look at it with my racing heart."

The race barely raised his pulse. "Even my heart rate, I was not nervous at all because, I don't know, it just felt kind of cruising out there, just trying to press the battery whenever it was correct or defend. But for the show, when you look at it from the outside, probably yes, you like it."

Verstappen currently sits second in the drivers' standings. Mercedes' Kimi Antonelli claimed the title at the Mexican Grand Prix but the fight for second will continue into the upcoming Qatar Grand Prix. Verstappen has a 37-point lead over Lewis Hamilton , who sits third. George Russell and Lando Norris are fourth and fifth, respectively, both on 264 points.

Isack Hadjar left the 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix empty-handed after a Lap 26 front wing failure and a post-race 10-second safety car penalty relegated him to 15th, yet Red Bull team principal Laurent Mekies saw something far more encouraging on the Strip.

Having qualified fourth and briefly led on Lap 9 during an early one–two run alongside eventual winner Max Verstappen , the 22-year-old earned glowing praise from Mekies - who believes Hadjar’s growing emotional composure is now translating directly into speed.

Asked in his post-race media session about the fearless approach Hadjar showed after declaring on Thursday he had "nothing to lose" in chasing a win across the final three rounds, Mekies credited both his pace and tactical discipline.

"He did very, very good, honestly," Mekies said. "He was there on merit; he was there because he had the pace; he was there because he was understanding very well the defensive, offensive manoeuvres, and that’s the best situation possible for us. And he also understood well when we asked him to keep the battles for later and to concentrate on not getting caught by the guys behind."

Mekies pointed to consecutive breakthrough weekends - including the Sao Paulo Grand Prix two weeks earlier, where Hadjar yielded second to a charging Verstappen before securing his third podium of 2026 - as proof of steady growth. "He was P4 on the grid, P1, P2 on the road," Mekies noted. "Last weekend, we did P2, P3, and we had to swap the cars last weekend. So it’s good news."

That calm marks a noticeable shift for a driver whose fiery temperament drew attention earlier in his first season with the senior team - from heated radio exchanges during a troubled Monaco podium run in June to an FIA warning in Austin last month after a frustrating clash with Alex Albon in practice. According to Mekies, keeping a cooler head is unlocking performance on both sides of the pitwall.

"Isack is a very intelligent individual, and it’s very difficult for us non-drivers to assess what drivers have to deal with in terms of management of their emotions when they are in the car," Mekies explained.

"He recognised that, he knows very well that managing these emotions better gives him lap time in the car and lap time outside of the car in terms of the quality of the communication with his engineers.

"And as you pointed out, he made very, very big steps in the last two, three races. And we see even more potential moving forward. So he’s exchanging better and better with his engineering crew. And he’s executing stronger when he’s able to keep his emotions under control."

With Red Bull separately admitting it owed Hadjar an apology over the sudden wing collapse - while stewards handed him a 10-second penalty for slowing roughly 100 km/h behind the safety car to create a double-stack gap for Verstappen - Las Vegas did little for his eighth-place tally of 122 points.

Still, with Red Bull defending a nine-point edge over McLaren for third in the constructors’ standings (440 to 431) entering Qatar and Abu Dhabi, Hadjar’s sharper execution could prove decisive.

What was really behind Las Vegas's 352-overtake F1 chaos

The Race23.11.2026 23:22 · источник: https://www.the-race.com/formula-1/f1-las-vegas-gp-352-overtakes-chaos-explained/

With a record-breaking 352 overtakes and 35 changes of the lead, it is all too easy to rubbish the Las Vegas Grand Prix as the perfect example of Formula 1 2026's fake 'yo-yo' racing.

Several drivers were quite dismissive of how an F1 race quite unlike anything seen before played out.

Race winner Max Verstappen ridiculed his experience as something more akin to a computer game.

"I remember winning one like this when I was playing Mario Kart," he told the media afterwards.

But to brush aside Las Vegas as pure gameplay, or put it in the same bracket of artificiality as the 'yo-yo' battles early this year, is to ignore the factors that made it a very different beast.

This was not about the phoniness of overtakes being a consequence of cars having offset energy management patterns - one driver burning battery on straight A while another saved their deployment for straight B - and endlessly swapping without effort as a result.

Instead, when you dig a bit deeper, this was a consequence of a host of more traditional factors creating a perfect storm that should put it more in the category of Monza in the early 1970s rather than the very worst of F1 2026.

'Yo-yo racing' has become almost a dirty phrase in F1 this year, referencing a style of racing that became typical at the start of the season.

While F1 chiefs celebrated those early battles because of the number of positional swaps they triggered, the too-frequent passing and repassing left drivers and a lot of fans unimpressed because the reality is that not all overtakes are equal.

These battles were simply the consequence of energy offsets between cars and were exaggerated in the early days of these rules by drivers and teams not fully optimising their energy management, so approaching tracks with different usage patterns.

Slower cars blasting past quicker rivals simply because their car had more energy, before getting repassed on the next straight, looked good on television but was not evidence of quality racing.

Last weekend's Las Vegas GP produced a spectacle that bore many similarities to the early 2026 battles, but something very different was at play.

Yes, energy offsets contributed to what happened, but they were not the main catalyst. This was more about the slipstream effect: an element that is an ever-present when cars race each other in close proximity.

Physics dictate that, in a straight line, the car behind has an advantage because it is not having to punch a hole in the air. Sometimes the gain is big, sometimes it is small. In Las Vegas, it was massive.

And while many bemoan racing gimmicks such as overtake mode and the previously used DRS, slipstream battles are something accepted, celebrated and understood at all levels of the sport. This isn't a unique phenomenon that had its peak at Monza in 1971, when the closest finish in F1 history was recorded with a top five covered by just 0.61 seconds at the flag.

As Audi's racing director Allan McNish said about the Las Vegas spectacle: "I thought it was like Formula Ford at the old Silverstone when I was watching it."

One key difference in Las Vegas compared to the early races was that the fights stayed frenetic throughout.

At the start of 2026, the yo-yo battles would play out in the opening laps - often because Ferrari got itself out of position by making good starts.

However, once a car had broken out of the one-second buffer needed for the pursuer to lose overtake mode, then the pack broke up.

Las Vegas was not like that because the tow effect was so big, so it was really difficult for cars to get any clean air.

A look at the gaps during the race would show George Russell come out on to the Las Vegas strip outside that magic one-second zone behind race leader Max Verstappen, sometimes up to 1.3 seconds adrift. But the tow was worth so much that he could still getting onto the Red Bull's tail or even make a pass at the end of the straight.

The Las Vegas track layout was also perfect for ensuring that this generation of cars stayed close together.

One of the paradoxes of F1 2026 is that, in a year where the main criticism of this ruleset is that the cars are too energy-starved, the best racing can be delivered at the tracks where the batteries are struggling the most to be recharged.

On the long Vegas straight, cars were quickly out of energy so were running on the internal combustion engine alone. This meant the drag ratio of the cars versus their power was high - and meant the straights were taking longer to get down so, time-wise, were offering a slipstream for longer.

Also, unlike Monza, Las Vegas has no high-speed corners where cars cannot follow each other easily. The slow nature of the twists and turns of Las Vegas meant cars could stay in touch with those ahead, meaning nobody could break free.

But you still needed some underlying pace to keep in touch with the cars ahead. Lose too much time on the straights or in the corners (like Aston Martin and Cadillac) and the train ahead was gone.

As Aston Martin's chief trackside officer Mike Krack said: "You could see also in our race, as soon as you lose the tow, it was going to be difficult. Especially with the disadvantage that we were having. If you have no tow, then it is long afternoon, long evening."

Feeding into all of this is that Las Vegas is not a track where tyre degradation is a major factor, so there was not the tyre offset between cars that happens at a lot of other venues.

Everybody could pretty much push as hard as they wanted and there was potentially even a factor of the cars that were sliding around a bit more, in the wake of those ahead, heating their rubber in the cold temperatures.

The racing was also assisted by the Las Vegas layout not prompting a huge divergence in energy deployment in terms of where to burn more battery.

While tracks such as Melbourne and Shanghai delivered plenty of different options in terms of straights that were deemed best to deploy on, Las Vegas was more restricted.

The divergence mainly seemed to be whether to save some deployment on the Strip for the run to the line, or burn it all at once. This explained a lot of the Verstappen shuffling.

While these energy offsets contributed to some of the overtaking, unlike Australia and China they were not the sole reason for so much passing. That again came down to the slipstream effect.

One element that leaves many drivers disliking yo-yo racing is that it is too random and the passes are nothing to do with clever thinking nor being better than your rivals.

Blasting past a rival on one straight because they are out of power, and then getting overtaken on the next straight because you are offset, means nothing.

Las Vegas was not like that. It called for some strategic thinking in terms of managing the racing and getting yourself into the position you needed to be in through the final corners.

For Williams's Carlos Sainz, who ended up beating Russell to the line for eighth, this was a race where drivers needed to be smart.

"I don't think there was that much yo-yo racing," he said. "It was more overtakes at the end of the straight, then you stay in front the next lap, and then the overtake mode, you get the overtake and then you pass back. I think it was very strategic.

"It reminded me a bit of a go-kart race with a long straight where you need to play with the slipstreams and in this case with the battery. I like strategic races."

McLaren's Lando Norris was the perfect example of how drivers had to work hard for a good result. His deployment was not perfect early on as he dropped into the pack.

But some thinking on the fly with McLaren - and up to nine switch changes per lap - hauled him back into contention and helped him claim his runner-up spot.

The Las Vegas GP played out very much in the style of an oval race, in that the passing and shuffling was all going to be the build-up to the crescendo of a dash to the line. A host of drivers were in theory in contention for the win until the end.

Verstappen knew that he did not want to fall back into the pack, as he felt it better to be in front on the final lap. His radio messages asking about the ideal strategy for the dash to the chequered flag showed this was not a race going to be decided by random factors.

Russell's off was the consequence of a risky move against Mercedes team-mate Kimi Antonelli because he felt that he needed to be second on the final lap.

Antonelli and Mercedes reckoned they had had something up their sleeve to win it at the end had Antonelli stayed close to Verstappen, before the world champion's lift off behind Verstappen confused his engine algorithms.

In the end, the drivers had a huge influence on the outcome: needing to know which battles to pick, where they needed to save and deploy energy, and make sure they were in the right position starting that final lap.

The spectacular final-corner fight at the front did not play out because of Antonelli's slip up. But Sainz pinching a place off Russell across the line was a taste of how things could have been.

Ultimately, it was Verstappen's determination to remain at the front of the train, rather than happily sit and wait, that triggered a lot of the passes and repassing that characterised the spectacle.

If he had been happy to sit and wait in the pack until the final lap then it could have been pretty static.

Statistics show in terms of mileage (not actual laps), Verstappen led 91% of the race distance - even if he did not enjoy it.

For McLaren team principal Andrea Stella, who had his own reservations about the safety element of cars battling like that for so long, the important thing now was for F1 to step back and understand the elements in play so it can deliver the right spectacle in the future.

"When you have examples like today, which offer so much information, it would be a shame to process it in the space of a hot review and say, 'Oh, let's abandon the new regulations'," he said.

"That's not the case, but definitely there's some interesting information that we should consider from a technical point of view, from a sporting regulation point of view."

The madness of the 2026 Las Vegas GP is not something to lambast and regret. It is something to learn from and can help F1 work out what type of racing it really wants in the future.

Ferrari team principal Frederic Vasseur says Charles Leclerc must shoulder the blame for colliding with his team mate in the Las Vegas Grand Prix.

The stewards ruled Leclerc was “wholly to blame” for the collision . Asked whether he held Leclerc solely responsible for the collision or divided the blame between his drivers, Vasseur said: “No, I think it was… The decision of the FIA, was quite clear.”

Leclerc backed out of an attempt to pass Hamilton in the high-speed turn 17 two laps before their collision. Vasseur indicated the team warned Leclerc to take care shortly before the collision.

“We passed a message to Charles five corners before,” he told Viaplay. “For me, it’s a professional mistake.

“I know that he apologised, I know he was sorry, but once again we destroyed the race today. We could have done much better. I don’t know what could be the outcome of the race because it was quite chaotic, but we can’t lose points like this.

“And more than points, it’s the motivation of everybody, the reward of the job done, the rewards of the effort and this is a huge frustration for me.”

Leclerc’s radio was largely silent between the collision on lap 38 and the end of the race. After he took the chequered flag, Vasseur told him: “Charles, I want to see you just after the race, before everything.”

Leclerc said Vasseur told him “that it was not acceptable again.”

“I think it’s been too many times,” he continued. “I took the 10 seconds penalty, which I guess – I mean, not ‘I guess’ – which means that I am predominantly at fault in this clash and I will take the blame for that.”

“The result is that today, because of something I did, we don’t score the points that we should have and that is not acceptable,” Leclerc added. “I will look back at the incident and try to understand what I could have done better, differently and to make sure that I don’t ever find myself in this situation again. F1 2027 grid: Who will be racing and where?

Autosport23.11.2026 21:09 · источник: https://www.autosport.com/f1/news/f1-2027-grid-who-will-be-racing-and-where/10847600/?utm_source=RSS&utm_medium=referral&utm_campaign=RSS-F1&utm_term=News&utm_content=uk

The 2027 Formula 1 grid has very much taken shape with all but one spot now confirmed following a rather safe ‘silly season’ that didn’t present much drama.

There is only one team - Haas - that is changing its line-up for next season as many potentially enthralling storylines failed to materialise in regards to the 2027 grid.

George Russell, Mercedes, Andrea Kimi Antonelli, Mercedes, Toto Wolff, Mercedes

Photo by: Hector Vivas / Formula 1 via Getty Images

The 2026 season has witnessed a significant shift in the dynamic down at Mercedes between Kimi Antonelli and George Russell, as the latter headed into the year as the heavy title favourite - only to fall flat early on.

It came after Russell finished 169 points ahead of Antonelli last season in what was a very up-and-down rookie campaign for the Italian, while the Briton arguably delivered his best yet. So, with Mercedes expected to master the regulation switch, which it did, Russell was the natural favourite yet Antonelli learned his lessons impressively fast to vastly improve upon 2025.

This eventually led to the 20-year-old becoming F1’s youngest ever world champion by securing his maiden title at the Mexico Grand Prix, with four rounds still to play at the time. Although this came as a surprise to many, Mercedes would have argued that his performances had been as forecast when it plucked him from karting and into its junior programme.

As for Russell, his season has been a “complete disaster” - per Max Verstappen - and with two grands prix left in 2026, he sits fourth in the standings and could easily drop to fifth as he is level on points with 2025 world champion Lando Norris .

Nevertheless, he will remain with Mercedes for 2027 with his and Antonelli’s fate known for quite some time.

Charles Leclerc, Ferrari, Lewis Hamilton, Ferrari

Photo by: Colin McMaster / LAT Images via Getty Images

Ferrari and Charles Leclerc agreed upon a new deal earlier in 2026 - announced in June - meaning the 29-year-old is continuing his length association with the Italian outfit. Leclerc joined its junior programme in 2016, made his F1 debut with customer squad Alfa Romeo Sauber in 2018 before moving to the works’ outfit in 2019.

The Monegasque has won nine grands prix in that time, which can be seen as a disappointment considering the promise displayed early on. But with Ferrari’s improved performance in 2026, jumping from fourth to second in the championship, Leclerc’s hope is that the team can press on and finally deliver a championship-winning car having signed a multi-year contract.

But he will have Lewis Hamilton to worry about in the neighbouring garage, as the seven-time world champion is understood to be under contract until the end of 2027 after joining in 2025. Last year was a disappointment for the Briton, finishing 86 points behind Leclerc, but he’s strengthened in 2026 with a 17-point advantage over his team-mate.

Tensions have also been high recently, with the two colliding on various occasions, most notably Monza, Mexico and Las Vegas, so that is a dynamic Ferrari must manage heading into 2027.

Oscar Piastri, McLaren, Lando Norris, McLaren

Photo by: Clive Rose / Formula 1 via Getty Images

It was known that at one stage Verstappen held some level of talks with McLaren when his Red Bull future was up in the air. Though this simply may have been a tactic to secure a more lucrative deal with the Austrian outfit, which is something he eventually earned, so McLaren is remaining with Norris and Oscar Piastri.

The Briton seems content with being a one-team man for the time being, staying with McLaren since his debut in 2019, and why not? Norris has secured that world title he so strived for and has been impressive in 2026 with three victories, even if the car hasn’t been quite there on several occasions.

As for Piastri, what happens with him is an on-and-off talking point given the Australian would have likely been the man to move had Verstappen joined. This year has been a disappointment for him, continuing on from his late title collapse in 2025, as he is seventh in the standings and 97 points behind Norris. His most recent victory was Zandvoort in August last year.

But he is on a “multi-year contract”, signed in 2025, and looks set to stay for now.

Max Verstappen, Red Bull Racing, Isack Hadjar, Red Bull Racing

The 2026 season has been quite similar to 2025 for Red Bull, having started quite slowly before a late rejuvenation with Verstappen enjoying four wins and nine podiums since this year’s summer break.

That has vaulted him into second in the drivers’ standings and should he carry such momentum into 2027, then a fifth world title could easily be on the way for the Dutchman. That would put him one short of Hamilton’s record for the most championships with one team - he achieved six at Mercedes - as Verstappen signed a bumper deal until at least 2030 this year.

It will see him start a second season alongside Isack Hadjar, who has had a solid first campaign at Red Bull and gave the team no reason to replace him. Perhaps the team now has its ideal number two driver to assist Verstappen in his title charge next year.

Arvid Lindblad, Racing Bulls, Liam Lawson, Racing Bulls

Photo by: Rudy Carezzevoli / Getty Images

The situation down at Red Bull’s sister outfit was one of the biggest sources of uncertainty, given it was only in November that Racing Bulls confirmed its pairing for 2027. It will be an unchanged duo, however, as Arvid Lindblad will return for his second season in F1 alongside Liam Lawson, whose seat was in doubt despite his best year yet.

Lawson is on course for a career-best ninth in the standings with 89 points, securing a top 10 finish at all but six grands prix, which in itself is enough for the Faenza outfit to secure its highest-ever constructors’ championship finish of fifth.

But Red Bull junior Nikola Tsolov has impressed as an F2 rookie this year, sitting second to Rafael Camara , and historically Racing Bulls doesn’t keep drivers for long if a promotion to the main squad isn’t on the cards.

With 2027 going to be Lawson’s third proper season at Racing Bulls, historically the team may have dropped him in favour of its next protege. Tsolov, however, will have to settle for a reserve driver role in 2027 while Lindblad continues to perform, taking 38 points from his rookie year so far and 11th in the standings.

Photo by: Lars Baron / LAT Images via Getty Images

Pierre Gasly signed a contract extension with Alpine last year, demonstrating his commitment to the cause despite the team being adrift at the back of the constructors' order across 2025. The arrival of Flavio Briatore, Steve Nielsen, and Mercedes has put the team back into the midfield battle, and the Frenchman is hoping that the Enstone squad can deliver a car worthy of his immense - and oft-underrated - talent.

Gasly is 10th in the 2026 standings with 52 points, helping Alpine sit sixth in the constructors’ championship and although he’s the French outfit’s clear number one driver, Franco Colapinto has still made a considerable step.

The Argentine has taken 35 points in 2026, compared to zero last year, and that earned him a new deal in August for 2027.

Oliver Bearman, Haas F1 Team, Rafael Camara, Haas F1 Team

Photo by: Bryn Lennon / Formula 1 via Getty Images

Haas is another squad to have endured serious question marks surrounding its driver pairing for next year. The first concerned Esteban Ocon , who just couldn’t make things click ever since he joined the American outfit from Alpine for 2025.

At the time he was seen as a real coup, the first grand prix winner to drive for Haas, but instead he was consistently outperformed by his much more inexperienced team-mate - Oliver Bearman - across both last year and 2026.

In the end things turned quite ugly and after it being confirmed that the 198-time grand prix starter wouldn’t continue into 2027, he was dismissed by Haas with five rounds still to play in the season.

His replacement is F2 championship leader Camara, who has signed a “multi-year” deal and given up his title ambitions in the feeder series to finish the season in F1 before his first, full campaign in 2027.

That will likely come alongside Bearman, who has mightily impressed in his time at Haas so far. But in Vegas, the 21-year-old Briton said he was “disappointed” to not yet have his contract wrapped up for 2027 with the reason believed to be a disagreement in salary. Bearman’s seat is the only one not confirmed for next year.

Gabriel Bortoleto, Audi F1 Team, Nico Hulkenberg, Audi F1 Team

Photo by: Guido De Bortoli / LAT Images via Getty Images

Audi F1 boss Mattia Binotto confirmed in September that it will retain Nico Hulkenberg and Gabriel Bortoleto for the 2027 season. At the time it didn’t really cause any particular conversations because the announcement came as little surprise, considering how well both drivers have done - thus giving Audi no reason to change its line-up.

The German marque, which made his F1 debut in 2026 after taking over from Sauber, has the perfect blend of youth and experience, plus a harmonious relationship between the pair.

Bortoleto is set for his third season in the championship, compared to Hulkenberg’s 16th, and both drivers have scored 14 points this year to give Audi eighth in the standings.

Alexander Albon, Williams, Carlos Sainz, Williams

Williams' decision to lock in Alex Albon and Carlos Sainz for at least another year demonstrates the buy-in for its long-term ambitions from both drivers, even if this season has presented a step back in fortunes. Some might uncharitably suggest that there was nowhere else for either driver to go, which is probably also true, but the retention of both drivers is a significant boost for the team.

Had Sainz decided to do something else, Williams is said to have hedged its bets and had spoken to the management of Sergio Perez and Colapinto for 2027, while Luke Browning has also impressed in his FP1 outings with the team.

Fernando Alonso, Aston Martin Racing, Lance Stroll, Aston Martin Racing

Photo by: Alastair Staley / LAT Images via Getty Images

The situation at Aston Martin was another which promised potential drama for 2027 but failed to materialise as such. That is because for months the questions surrounding Fernando Alonso’s future were strife, considering he is 45 years old, driving for a backmarker and heavily dislikes the current regulations with his previous contract expiring at the end of 2026.

But all speculation stopped in September when the two-time champion announced his extension with Aston Martin into 2027. It is believed that him not publicly committing to F1 through the summer months was partly an effort to put pressure on the series to make changes.

As for team-mate Lance Stroll, his extension into 2027 arrived on the same day as Alonso, but was far from the same talking point considering father Lawrence owns the team. So, the 28-year-old Canadian, who next season will have had a decade in F1, effectively has a contract for as long as he likes.

Valtteri Bottas, Cadillac, Sergio Perez, Cadillac

F1 newcomer Cadillac was the final squad to hold intrigue surrounding its driver pairing for 2027. Said intrigue didn’t really concern Sergio Perez, who in October signed a multi-year extension, but more Valtteri Bottas.

The 10-time grand prix winner has been outperformed by Perez this year, with a losing 16-10 head-to-head record in qualifying (including sprints), causing his seat to be thrown in doubt. Team management refused to publicly commit to him for some time as it weighed up the possibility of promoting reserve driver Colton Herta, given its desire to have an American driver racing for an American team.

One sticking point for Herta was his lack of an FIA Superlicence - required to compete in F1 - despite winning nine races in IndyCar. But he eventually obtained one via six FP1 appearances this year, yet that still wasn’t enough to grab an F1 seat with his highly disappointing F2 season, sitting 19th in the standings, in mind.

So, in November Cadillac announced its decision to stick with Bottas for 2027.

Formula 1 is planning a shift to just two power unit supplies per manufacturer in 2030, Autosport has learned, raising questions over the status of Mercedes' three current customers.

Over the Las Vegas weekend the FIA announced there was unanimous support to bring the next generation of power unit regulations forward to 2030. That includes a move to 2.1-litre V8 engines, including a turbocharger, a maximum of 200 kilowatts of electric power and the use of sustainable fuels.

That move satisfies the demand for cheaper and less complex engines, which FIA president Mohammed Ben Sulayem and F1 chief Stefano Domenicali had been pursuing in unison. Meanwhile, adding a turbo was a key requirement for the likes of Audi and also ensures engine noise remains at a suitable level for some of F1's urban venues.

What was also part of a wide-ranging document the FIA produced on the 2030 framework, but wasn't part of the public announcement, is an agreement between the six 2030 power unit manufacturers to supply just two teams each. This also includes their works team, leaving just one single engine supply available for external customer teams.

Autosport understands that one aim of the move is to reduce the political influence of manufacturers supplying a large share of the grid, in this case Mercedes and Ferrari , while the manufacturers in question had also been willing to downsize as supplying F1 engines is a loss-making operation, with supplies capped at just $18m per season.

Ferrari is currently supplying the works team, Haas and Cadillac, but the latter will be moving to in-house engines from 2030, meaning the question of Ferrari's supply is solving itself. Red Bull Powertrains will also be taking care of two teams, Red Bull Racing and Racing Bulls , while at present Audi, Aston Martin partner Honda and Cadillac's shareholder General Motors will all have their own works teams as their one and only confirmed customer.

That leaves the question of what will happen to Mercedes and the customers of Mercedes High Performance Powertrains. Alongside the works team in Brackley, the Brixworth operation is currently supplying McLaren , Williams as well as Alpine , whose parent company Renault recently abandoned and sold its in-house engine operation.

Per the 2030 framework, two of those three teams would be looking for a new engine supply if the FIA World Motor Sport Council approves the proposal in Shanghai next month. That seismic shift will set up months of political intrigue in the F1 paddock as teams scramble to secure what has been F1's most reliable and performant power unit supplier ever since F1 moved to hybrid engines in 2014, or conclude deals with currently less desirable options.

Autosport understands that both McLaren and Williams are fancying their chances of remaining with Mercedes. And while McLaren at least explored the idea of becoming its own power unit manufacturer when a move to a cheaper formula would make that more feasible than it is now, CEO Zak Brown indicated that ship has sailed for 2030 as he aims to remain a Mercedes customer.

"It's something we've looked at in the past, but we're very happy with HPP," Brown said in Las Vegas on Thursday. "I think we've proven that we can win championships with them, so we don't have any intention to have anything in the back of our car other than a Mercedes."

Mercedes' intentions about what its ideal customer team would look like are less clear right now. Having been beaten by McLaren in 2024 and 2025, CEO Toto Wolff maintained that a win for a customer is still a feather in the cap for HPP and the Mercedes brand as a whole, while a strong customer team also provides a valuable chassis benchmark for the works operation.

And while Brown hailed HPP as an "awesome partner", it's no secret that the relationship between HPP and McLaren has at least come under some strain at the start of this year amid a knowledge gap between the works team and its customer over how to exploit and deploy the complex new power units.

Whether Mercedes will continue to lease engines to its most formidable rival or whether a deal with a midfield team is the preferred option will likely become the subject of plenty of intrigue in the coming months.

What is certain is that the FIA's proposed third-party engine plan is now dead in the water. With F1 power units becoming less complex and expensive in 2030, FIA president Ben Sulayem had floated the idea of sourcing a supply of off-the-shelf, white-label engines to further alleviate concerns over team independence.

However, that plan never seemed economically viable for any prospective engine builder, and with all six PUMs at the table now unanimously agreeing to 2030's key infrastructure, a cap of two supplies would mean there are at least 12 power unit supplies available for 11 teams.

The FIA did include a safety net in case one of the six manufacturers decides to drop out, adding a clause under which a departing manufacturer’s F1 power unit intellectual property would transfer to the FIA, which could then assign it to a new manufacturer coming in.

As is the case today, there will also be an "obligation to supply", meaning the FIA could force one of the manufacturers to supply an additional, third team with an engine if that outfit can't arrange a deal to find one, which may well end up becoming a key clause.

What has also been agreed is the basis for the power unit cost cap for the 2030-2034 period. The cap will remain at today's figure of $190m for 2030 before falling by $7.5m per year, ending at $160m for the 2034 season.

Existing manufacturers will also receive more cost-cap headroom than newcomer General Motors, which can now abandon its V6 plans and just focus on the V8 programme in Charlotte. GM is understood to be signing up to a budget cap of $170m for the first couple of years.

There are still several important topics to be fleshed out further by the manufacturers, such as the exact nature of the electric push-to-pass system as it would need to work in conjunction with the chassis design to promote close racing.

But by defining the overarching framework and key engine architecture, which will be rubber-stamped by the FIA World Motor Sport Council, both the manufacturers and the FIA can now get to work on the next rules cycle on a reasonable timeline.

That should help the governing body come up with chassis regulations which are tuned to work well with the new power units and deliver a more pure and exciting set of rules, without facing the prospect of having to adapt to late U-turns by OEMs, which led to 2026's compromised and criticised formula to begin with.

Isack Hadjar has received a 10-second penalty for driving unnecessarily slowly in Formula 1’s Las Vegas Grand Prix – the result of his actions under the safety car.

The race was neutralised on lap 19 when Sergio Perez and Rafael Camara collided; Max Verstappen was leading team-mate Hadjar but the Red Bulls and Mercedes cars lay within just over a second; the double-stack meant Hadjar risked losing track position to Russell and other drivers.

The Frenchman therefore slowed down, to the extent that he was 4.288s slower than his team-mate on that lap. This mitigated the effect of the double-stack, though he still couldn’t retain second position.

Hadjar has now landed a 10-second penalty, whose impact actually is marginal, dropping him from 14th to 15th after his front wing broke at the restart.

“While driving on the straight between Turns 13 and 14 under Safety Car conditions, the driver of Car 6 received repeated instructions from his team stating ‘five seconds, five seconds, five seconds’,” the stewards’ report read.

“Following these instructions, Car 6 significantly reduced its speed, at one point travelling approximately 100km/h slower than Car 3, which was ahead of it. The Stewards determined that this significant reduction in speed was deliberate and intended to create a gap to the car ahead and was not made for safety reasons, thereby allowing the team to perform a double-stack pitstop without Car 6 losing time waiting behind its team-mate in the pitlane.

“Furthermore, this manoeuvre directly impacted the cars following Car 6, which were forced to reduce their speed accordingly and could not overtake Car 6 due to the restrictions applicable under Safety Car conditions.

“The Stewards considered that Car 6 was driving unnecessarily slowly for tactical reasons and that such conduct constituted a breach of the applicable regulations. In reaching their decision, the Stewards also took into account the precedent involving Car 4 (Norris) at the 2023 Canadian Grand Prix, where a penalty was imposed for similar conduct.”

The aforementioned clash, with Perez spinning Camara around and breaking his Cadillac’s suspension, earned the Mexican a five-place grid penalty for the Qatar round.

“Car 11 (PER) attempted to overtake Car 51 (CAM) on the inside, on the left-hand side, approaching Turn 14,” the stewards wrote.

“The Stewards noted that at no point during the manoeuvre was the front axle of Car 11 at least alongside the mirror of Car 51. The two cars collided at the entry to Turn 14, resulting in Car 11 retiring from the Race.

“Applying the Driving Standards Guidelines, the Stewards determined that Car 11 had not earned the right to the corner and was wholly to blame for the collision.”

Ferrari team boss Fred Vasseur says Charles Leclerc's contact with Lewis Hamilton in the Las Vegas Grand Prix was "unacceptable" and showed "lack of respect for the team", with the Formula 1 team set for fresh crisis talks just three weeks after their spat in Mexico.

Vasseur had to hold talks with both Leclerc and Hamilton for their costly Mexico skirmish and will do so again after his two cars tangled on the streets of Las Vegas on lap 38, with Leclerc to blame for leaving his front wing dangling on the inside of Hamilton, sending the Briton into a spin.

Leclerc received a 10-second penalty for the collision, while Hamilton dropped to 10th and eventually crossed the line in eighth. Leclerc was classified in 10th place after the penalty had been added to his race time.

Coming so soon after Mexico, the thought of his drivers tangling again and the prospect of fresh talks to clear the air had worn thin on Vasseur.

"Charles, I want to see you at the end of the race, before anything else," Vasseur said on the team radio during the cooldown lap.

Winners and losers from F1's 2026 Las Vegas Grand Prix

The Race22.11.2026 13:22 · источник: https://www.the-race.com/formula-1/f1-2026-las-vegas-grand-prix-winners-losers/

A relentless lead battle was the defining feature of the Las Vegas Grand Prix, but the way the drivers involved in it dealt with it varied drastically.

Here's our pick of winners and losers from Formula 1's most entertaining Vegas race yet:

On a day of frantic wheel-to-wheel racing there seemed almost an inevitability that the drivers most likely to hit trouble would be Ferrari team-mates Lewis Hamilton and Charles Leclerc.

Having hauled themselves into the train of cars at the front, frustrations were growing between the pair about battling each other prior to Hamilton picking up his 10-second penalty for cutting the track in a fight with Lando Norris.

But even after that, the pair ended up needlessly colliding - dumping Hamilton down the order and earning Leclerc a penalty.

After all the public talk of things being rosy between the Ferrari duo after their Mexico incident, Las Vegas has shown there is still a lot that needs to be fixed. - Jon Noble

Contending for the win but inferior in battle, and ending the race in the wall, Vegas just about sums up Russell's season.

His battle with Verstappen felt a bit like a much, much younger brother trying to wrestle a much older, bigger one. Even when Russell looked to have the upper hand, Verstappen stormed back with ease. And the rest of the time Russell looked a bit hapless in the face of adversity.

On his crash, while trying to repass team-mate Kimi Antonelli, he said: "I knew if I wanted to win the race, I needed to be in second going into the last couple of laps.

"Kimi didn't have the overtake mode and I went for a high-risk move and paid the price. But I don't regret it. Of course, I'm disappointed with it. But I could have been conservative and I would have finished in third."

The fact that he crashed trying to even get in a position to fight Verstappen lends some credence to the Red Bull driver's assessment that Russell is not on his level is true, even if we add the caveat that that's as things stand this year. - Jack Benyon

In such a chaotic race with karting-levels of passes for the lead, it's little surprise that a racing animal such as Verstappen emerged on top.

Verstappen judged things beautifully, keeping a calm head, outdriving and out-strategising both Mercedes drivers for a fourth victory in six races. - JS

It all seemed to be coming to Antonelli as his offset strategy that put him on softs for the final stint appeared to leave him as Verstappen's main threat for the win, as part of a battle that would play out in the dash to the line.

But in a year where he has been brilliant in managing the quirks of these 2026 power units, Antonelli got it wrong.

Having lifted off to keep the ideal gap to Verstappen, that ended up messing up his deployment - as the response from the energy-management algorithms was to burn too much power when he didn't want it to be used up.

The result was a loss of momentum to McLaren duo Lando Norris and Oscar Piastri that meant he missed out on a podium on a day when he had it in his hands to pull off another brilliant win. - JN

Considering McLaren had "horrendous" straightline speed in Las Vegas, Norris and Piastri earning a double podium is a fantastic overachievement for McLaren.

They both drove really clever races despite that weakness, making the most of running in the train and being smart with where they made their overtakes.

That was particularly encouraging for Piastri, who was able to charge from 17th to third for his first grand podium since Miami in early-May. - JS

As high as third in the early running, Lindblad later committed one of racing's faux pas by spinning under the safety car.

He said he'd had a weird shift which was "the strangest thing I've experienced", but did caveat that he wanted to take the blame for what had happened.

His biggest regret? "I can race for P8, P9, P10 in the next couple of races. That's fantastic. Not often you get to race for P3, P4, P5 in a midfield car in your rookie season. I think that's what hurts me."

Lindblad felt he missed a similar chance in Mexico qualifying recently. You can tell he's desperate for a headline performance to back up what has been a strong season that many people haven't yet noticed. - JB

It still feels like fifth place in the constructors' championship is out of reach, but Alpine couldn't have done much more in Las Vegas, as both Racing Bulls cars hit trouble.

Pierre Gasly ran as high as third, and pulled off a majestic drive to fifth fighting Antonelli to the flag, despite running 45 of the 50 laps with the power turned down due to an overheating issue.

Alpine will review that, but ultimately Gasly was happy even if at the point he reached third he felt he could fight for the win.

Franco Colapinto's race was much more of a rollercoaster, and he needed some help from late issues from various drivers including Charles Leclerc (penalty) and Russell (crash to finish sixth).

But he recovered from having no grip on used softs early on - he gained three places at the start but lost those quickly - to a sure-thing top 10 regardless of the issues of those ahead.

The constructors' points gap to Racing Bulls is 27 with two races to go. - JB

This was the first race where it really felt like Hadjar was a threat for victory, and it fell apart in catastrophic circumstances as his front wing buckled right after the restart following the early safety car.

If you expected him to be sombre after the race, his comments were borderline troubling.

"It's not even about the points or the podium, it's just ruining my fun," he said.

"I'm here to race the best guys in the world and I end up at the back and having to race alone. It's just ruining my motivation to drive. It's just so annoying. I guess I never know what kind of result I could have had."

But you can choose to take a much more positive view of this. He suggested what would have arguably been a better race strategy, to keep swapping positions at the front to try and build a gap, which Verstappen seemingly vetoed on track. He was the most threatening for a win that he's looked so far too and, as in Baku, he showed on these sorts of street circuits that he's a proper contender.

A loser from the result, but certainly not in performance. - JB

There will have been an awful lot of people looking at the timing tower at the end of the race asking: how on earth did Carlos Sainz end up eighth?

The bedrock of that was an impressive set of opening laps as Sainz went from 18th to 11th.

Thereafter, he kept sight of the leading pack after the safety car, then picked off a baulked Russell (who was caught behind Hamilton) with an opportunistic move just before the line to take a season's-best eighth, far above where the current Williams should be. - JS
